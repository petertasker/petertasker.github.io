Dark patterns, which are design choices that deceive or manipulate users, usually for the benefit of a system with opposing interests, have received increasing academic attention (Tasker et al. 2026). Bright patterns, which act in users’ interests where those interests diverge from the system’s, are a more recent topic of discussion. Nudges, meanwhile, are design choices that steer users towards options that serve them. 

I should say at the outset that these are working definitions, and that they are contested. The HCI community uses “dark pattern”, “bright pattern” and “nudge” to describe interface patterns, particularly in the context of privacy, user harm and user agency, but researchers apply the terms inconsistently. Some treat nudges as the mechanism through which dark or bright patterns operate. Others use “nudge” in a value-negative, value-neutral or value-positive sense, and others stretch it beyond system-to-individual influence to a sociological one, such as nudging a research field towards a particular area, sometimes all within the same paper. The connotations are just as varied. Definitions of dark patterns variously hinge on the manipulative or deceptive nature of the design, or on the opposing interests of the user and the interface, and some papers distinguish an interface from the (often corporate) interests that deploy it while others do not. English is not a formal system, so this leaves otherwise well-meaning readers open to misinterpretation. The definitions above are therefore one reading among several, and I use “nudge” here in its value-positive sense. I am working on the underlying problem separately, by examining how the terms are defined in the literature and asking researchers which definitions they agree with.

There is a second term I have been using loosely: “good”. Whether a pattern is dark, bright or a nudge depends on whether it serves the user, and that depends on what we take “good” to mean. For now, I define good as a person’s ability, in their capacity as a user, to make informed, autonomous decisions when interacting with an interface, in a way that serves the interests they hold as a user to the greatest feasible extent, for the greatest number of users with aligned interests. The phrase “in their capacity as a user” matters. The CEO of Amazon is a person, but their interests as CEO and their interests as a shopper on Amazon can diverge, and only the second counts here. Interests attach to roles, not to people. This definition is provisional, and several of its parts are open to challenge: it assumes that “the user” can be identified, that interests can be aligned across users, and that informed, autonomous choice is the right measure of a good outcome. The questions above about averaging users, and about who decides what counts as someone’s interest, are questions about this definition.

At first, the distinction between bright patterns and nudges may seem narrow at best and muddy at worst. I suggest the difference lies in who bears the cost: a bright pattern works against the system’s own interest on the user’s behalf, whereas a nudge need not. Both, however, involve someone deciding what is good for you.

There is a complication here. If a bright pattern steers users without their knowing, it is hard to say it is not deceptive, however kind its motive. By the definition above, that would make it a dark pattern with good intentions. Perhaps what separates the two is not intent alone but whether the steering can be seen, understood and refused. I raise this now because it threatens the line I have just drawn, and I would rather leave it unsettled than defend it too early.

That raises a key question: do people want to be treated paternalistically? I focus here on software design, but the question applies equally to governance, infrastructure and education. At what point does a person say, “Stop: I will decide for myself now”? One pole is full autonomy, the other full paternalism, and most designs sit somewhere between.

And what do we mean by “the user”? Individuals are not a monolith. One user may want transparent, pro-consumer software; another may accept reduced agency in exchange for protection from harm; another may want to be deceived outright. If we averaged these preferences, would atypical users simply register as noise? Averaging risks erasing exactly the people for whom a default is most harmful.

This leads to a broader question: how do upbringing and society shape our reception of paternalistic design? Do age, political belief, cultural heritage or material circumstances change how someone responds to benevolent paternalism? Does their view of paternalism in wider society, such as their opinion of states with different levels of intervention, colour their view of paternalistic systems?

Two further questions sit behind that word. First, who is the paternalist? A company, a regulator, an open-source maintainer and an AI agent acting on your behalf are not equivalent, and I suspect that how far we trust the one doing the steering shapes how we feel about being steered. Second, who decides what counts as the user’s interest? Interests conflict across time (what I want now against what I will want in a year) and between users, and whoever draws the line is exercising exactly the authority in question.

There is also a caution about what such findings could show. People who live under heavy paternalism may come to approve of it, which philosophers call adaptive preference. If so, discovering that a group accepts paternalistic design tells us how they feel, not whether the design is justified. Measuring attitudes does not settle the ethics, and I want to keep the two apart.

Consider Dostoyevsky's Underground Man, who insists on his freedom to act against his own interest, even when it gains him nothing, because doing so asserts his agency. Others, by contrast, knowingly seek out deception. A magic trick is the clearest case: the audience pays to be fooled, and the performance fails if they are not. It is rarely harmful, but it is deception of a kind. People may even enjoy being unable to explain what they saw, setting aside some of their agency and letting the performer steer their attention. Is that really deception, though, if it is consented to? The audience agrees to the outcome, but not to the method, and the method is misdirection, which is manipulation of attention by design. And might the choice architecture around giving up that agency itself be deceptive? Would people choose differently if they understood the choice better, or if it were presented differently?

I do not yet have answers to these questions, and I do not want to pretend otherwise. What I want to understand is how the societies and communities people identify with, and the way they were raised within them, shape their reception of paternalistic design. These operate at several levels. At a broad level, someone raised in a society that values autonomy over safety may respond very differently from someone raised where collective protection comes first. At a smaller level, self-organising communities, such as an open-source project, a faith group or an online forum, carry their own norms about who may steer whom. People also belong to several of these at once, so I do not treat any single affiliation, and certainly not nationality, as a predictor. I suspect the answer also depends on who the paternalist is, whether the steering is disclosed or hidden, and what is at stake. Those are the threads I intend to follow, and I expect the reasons people give for accepting or resisting being steered to matter as much as any pattern in the numbers.
