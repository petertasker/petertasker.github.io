I have been researching [dark patterns](https://deceptive.design/). Recently, a push has been made to reframe the *dark pattern* as a *deceptive pattern*, a *deceptive design*, or a permutation of these words. I will say here that I *like* this reframing: it is more descriptive to the average reader. When I had first heard the term *dark pattern* be used to describe some Facebook practice or other, it sounded so sinister that I had to look up what it meant---system design choices which influence users into making decisions they otherwise would not make. Sounds quite dark, and quite Facebook. 

The chief culprit of this reframing is the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM), on their [Words Matter](https://www.acm.org/diversity-inclusion/words-matter) article. I think words matter, and I support equity, diversity, and indeed inclusion. Let us now review some of the terms ACM has flagged as "impactful on people who hear or read them":

>>> **binary classification:** a machine learning technique that categorizes data into one of two groups based on a set of attributes. Binary versus non-binary can be charged terms for persons who identify either as binary or as non-binary, particularly if one identity (e.g., binary) is more common than another (e.g., non-binary) which may “normalize” one and “otherize” the other. We suggest using two-class classification instead.

Here, ACM has a problem with the word *binary*. They don't have any other reference to binaries in their article. But the idea of "binary" in computer science is not limited to only machine learning. Indeed, computers are *binary* systems, computer logic requires *binary*, or boolean, logic. Pluto and its moon Charon is a *binary* system. Binary is two-class by its definition. Here, the process of ML classification is "othering" things: it's either 𝑥, or it's not. But *not* 𝑥 is still the result of a binary classification! In other words, in ML, something can't fall outside of a binary classification. It either *is* or it *isn't*. The only similarity it shares with the nature of human gender is its name. It has nothing at all to do with non-binary people, who identify outwith the male / female binary. 

>>> **byzantine:** used to describe a condition of a (distributed) computer system where components may fail and there is imperfect information on whether a component has failed. The term reflects a prejudice against and erasure of cultures associated with and drawing from Byzantine civilization and endorses an exclusionary and denigrating stereotype against people of such cultural background. Alternatives for the problem name include: interactive consistency failure, source incongruency. For the adjective, some options are: faulty, corrupted, malicious, deviant, deceptive, demonic, perfidious, inconsistent, unreliable.

I think the Byzantines are sick, and I also happen to think that they *were* Rome. However, the Byzantine empire officially ended 600 years ago---I think we are not at risk of offending any Byzantines. Furthermore, the Byzantines were (in notoriously Italian fashion, further evidencing my belief that Byzantium was Rome) properly bad at bureaucracy. It was a centralised mess of power, Kafkaesque in conception, Machiavellian in execution. It is a great historic metaphor of needless convolution. Both claimants to the Byzantine throne, the [Ottomans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_claim_to_Roman_succession) and the [Russians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow,_Third_Rome), cherish / cherished their Byzantine descendancy. They (in their minds) are the descendants of the greatest ever empire! 

Again, if we are to not use Byzantine as an adjective, where will it stop? Must all negative historic allegories be stopped? I enjoy using the phrase "Pyrrhic victory." Might I have to stop using that lest a Tunisian get offended? We should cherish not only the history, real or mythological, but also the rich imagery that we inherit, however barbaric or morally egregious the circumstances were. 

In an interview with ACM, Byzantine historian (he is a historian of the Byzantines, not a complex, bureaucratic man) Anthony Kaldellis says:

>>> It’s not only the meaning of Byzantine as something excessively complicated or convoluted which is a meaning that has only really been in use for 80 years. This reaches back to medieval times when Western Europeans would call the Greeks treacherous and unreliable and deviant and effeminate...

*This* is offensive! This does matter. However, this word is not used to describe Greeks anymore, especially in the field of CS. We have better words to describe the Greeks now, especially post-debt crisis. ACM is a computing society, so it deals lightly with politics. But under this logic, if it were a historic society, the following would have to be thrown out the window: Gothic, Philistine, Luddite, Draconian, Vandal, Iconoclastic, etc.

Not all of ACM's definitions, alternatives, or reasonings are without merit---*Dark pattern* is very indescriptive, and *deceptive design* is, in a word, more suitable. The gendered language of ports and connectors is quite seedy and its male / female metaphor makes me a bit squeamish. I think describing an M/F port as a "gender bender" is in very poor taste! These are good, but once you slip into condemning "man in the middle attack", "black market", "subjects", and others you end up losing descriptiveness. In fact, you go the other way. We all know what a black market is, and we all know what a subject is. *Subject* is the subject of this sentence! You start to lose people with this axing of the English language. You ignore and subvert idioms. Language is very sensitive: an English speaker can hear a bastardised sentence like a deer can see movement in the forest. People are just trained to do it. When I read "attacker in the middle", I am taken completely unawares, and instead of focusing on cyber security, I focus on the choice of word attacker instead of man. I start to think of the lack of balance in the phrase "attacker in the middle". Phonology is the rules of the sound of a language. For example, the English language doesn't have the "ts" sound typically found in Slavic languages. Say the phrase "the Tsar" out loud. You omit the "t" and say "the 'sar". The same is true for sentences. We expect sentences to have a stress and an intonation. "Man in the middle": one, two, three, four. It's meat and potatoes. "Man" transforms into a discordant triplet calling itself an "attacker". At this point I've spent 30 seconds thinking about the jarringness of the phrase, and I have to restart the paragraph.

I'm playing the game, and I want to be published by ACM. But it's important to respect language. It's okay to use idioms or metaphors that have *contested* understandings. It is also okay to not. ACM here are publishing advice, not a degree. You can acknowledge the unsavoury history of these words---it's probably for the best. But let's be sensible about words. Words do matter, and changing words obstructs the public from education---one of ACM's core values.
